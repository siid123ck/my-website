Trek Nepal with confidence

Nepal offers some of the world's most rewarding treks, but permits, seasons, and costs can be confusing.

This guide covers the essentials in one place so you can plan with confidence, whether you are walking to Poon Hill for the first time or preparing for Everest Base Camp.

Choosing a Trek

Find the right route for your fitness, time, and interests.

New trekkers should start with Ghorepani Poon Hill (4 to 6 days) or Annapurna Base Camp (10 to 12 days). Both have well-marked trails and plenty of teahouses.

Experienced hikers looking for a challenge can choose Everest Base Camp or the Gokyo Lakes. Use the route list in Plan Your Trek to compare seasons.

Permits and TIMS Card

Most treks need permits. Arrange them before you set out.

The TIMS (Trekkers' Information Management System) card is a registration card required on most trekking routes.

Annapurna routes need an ACAP permit, Everest routes need a Sagarmatha National Park permit plus a local municipality permit, and Upper Mustang needs a restricted area permit with a registered guide.

Fees change, so confirm current prices with official offices in Kathmandu or Pokhara. Carry copies of your passport and permits.

Guides and Porters

Decide whether you want help on the trail.

Popular routes like Poon Hill and Annapurna Base Camp are well marked, so a guide is optional. Remote routes like Gokyo or Upper Mustang are safer with one.

A porter carries your main bag, usually up to about 20 kg. Hire through a licensed agency, check the guide's credentials, and pay fair wages.

Best Seasons

Choose a season for clear views and safe trails.

Autumn (September to November): the clearest skies and the most popular season. Book teahouses early.

Spring (March to May): warm days, blooming rhododendrons, and good visibility.

Winter (December to February): cold at altitude and snow on high passes, but quieter trails and clear lower-altitude views.

Monsoon (June to August): rain and landslide risk on most routes. Upper Mustang is the exception, since it lies in a rain shadow.

Altitude Sickness Safety

Acclimatize slowly and learn the warning signs.

Altitude sickness can affect anyone above about 2,500 m (8,200 ft). The rule is "climb high, sleep low." Avoid raising your sleeping altitude by more than about 500 m per day, and take a rest day every few days.

Mild symptoms include headache, nausea, dizziness, and poor sleep. If they get worse, stop climbing and descend. Confusion, breathlessness at rest, or a cough with fluid are emergencies: descend immediately.

Check that your travel insurance covers helicopter evacuation, and speak to a doctor before you go about preventive medication.

Packing List

Pack light, in layers, and keep everything waterproof.

Dress in layers: a base layer, fleece, down jacket, and waterproof shell. Add sturdy broken-in boots, a warm hat, gloves, and a headlamp.

Use the interactive checklist in Plan Your Trek to tick off each item as you pack.

Teahouse Lodges

Simple rooms and home-cooked meals along the trail.

Teahouses offer basic rooms, often with shared bathrooms. Rooms are usually inexpensive or free when you eat your meals there.

Hot showers, device charging, and Wi-Fi usually cost extra. Meals typically cost a few hundred rupees each, and the menu is limited, so order early.

ATMs are scarce on the trail, so bring enough cash. Pack earplugs and a water filter or purification tablets.

Budget Planning

Estimate daily costs for food, lodging, permits, and transport.

As a rough guide, a teahouse trek costs about USD 30 per day on a budget, USD 50 mid-range, and USD 80 or more for a comfortable trip. These figures exclude permits, flights, and guides.

Other costs include permits, the flight or bus to the trailhead, a guide or porter if you hire one, and travel insurance. Use the estimator in Plan Your Trek to calculate your trip.
